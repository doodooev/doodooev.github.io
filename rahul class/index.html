Frederic Chopin (1810–1849) and Franz Liszt (1811–1886) were almost exact contemporaries, and both built their reputations in the Paris of the 1830s. This essay examines two works by each composer: Chopin’s Nocturne Op. 9 No. 2, and Ballade No. 1 in G minor; Liszt’s Liebestraum No. 3 and his famous “La Campanella”.

Chopin: Nocturne in E-flat major, Op. 9 No. 2

This nocturne is perhaps the best known of all Chopin’s short pieces. He wrote it around the age of 20 between 1830 and 1833. Three devices dominate the piece.


The first is the cantabile melody. The opening phrase is simple. It is notated to be played gently and softly. We can call it the “A” section. As we proceed forward into the piece, we notice the same section is further and further ornamented. Chopin would instruct his students to practice the left hand of the piece over and over. He would instruct them to get the left hand down and steady, while the right hand that plays the main melody of the piece can sway back and fourth with rubato, controlling the entire piece.

Here we can hear Section A played just as the first few bars, but ornamented. This is bar 13 of the piece, right before this we have section B , where we are instructed to slow down. Chopin marks bar 13 with “forzando piano” where we are instructed to play with a sudden rise in volume and then slowly drop down again.

The entire piece follows the structure of A – A – B- A – B – A followed by a beautiful coda towards the end. If we talk about various interpretations of this piece; you have pianists like Valentina Lisita or Dmitry Shishkin. Their interpretations do an amazing job of following the sheet music, playing softly with rubato. However, the most interesting interpretation, in my opinion, comes from one of Chopin’s own students: Raoul Koczalski. His playing features a great deal of improvisation, often straying far from the original score, including passages with improvised thirds.
